Leads into estimates

Are Facebook leads worth it for a tree service company?

By Zachary Spencer, founder of Zenith Co.
Published October 5, 2026

Short answer

They can be, but not the way most tree companies use them. Facebook leads come from people who weren't searching for a tree company, so many fill out the form and forget about it. They pay off when someone reaches each one within minutes and books a time on the spot, and when you pay for estimates that happen rather than for every form.

Ask ten tree company owners about Facebook leads and most will say the same thing: tire kickers. Half don't answer the phone. The other half wanted a price over text for a job nobody has seen. That experience is real. It just isn't the whole story, and the reasons behind it are fixable.

Why Facebook leads feel like tire kickers

The difference is where the homeowner was when they saw you. Someone who searches "tree removal near me" on Google was already looking for a tree company. Someone scrolling Facebook was looking at their cousin's vacation photos. Your ad reminded them about the oak over the roof, and a lead form that takes a few taps caught them in that moment.

That makes the lead real but fragile. The interest is genuine for a few minutes. Then they put the phone down, dinner happens, and by tomorrow the form they filled out feels like something they did by accident. Call them the next afternoon and you get voicemail, or "oh, we're just looking."

What makes them worth it

1. Reach them while they're still holding the phone

The single biggest difference is speed. A text in your company's name within a minute or two of the form, followed by a call, reaches the homeowner while the tree is still on their mind. The same lead called back the next day is a different person. How fast is fast enough.

2. Turn the form into a time, not a callback

"Someone will call you to schedule" loses people. "We can come look Thursday at 9 or Friday at 2. Which works?" gets an answer. The goal of the first conversation is a booked estimate on your calendar, with a reminder before the visit. A Facebook lead that books a time stops being a tire kicker and becomes an appointment.

3. Ask enough to sort them

A form that only asks for a name and number can't tell a removal from a hedge trim, or a homeowner in your county from one two hours away. Asking what the job is and where it is lets you skip the ones you'd never take before anyone drives anywhere.

4. Pay for the right thing

If you pay per lead, every tire kicker costs you money. If you pay a retainer, they cost you money and patience. The tire kicker problem mostly goes away when the fee is tied to estimates where the homeowner actually showed up. That's how we bill: you pay your ad spend on your own card, and our fee only applies to shown estimates. How that works.

Which tree jobs suit Facebook ads

Facebook is good at jobs people already know they need but haven't gotten around to: trimming limbs over the roof, palm cleanups, stump grinding, the dead tree in the back corner. Seeing a local company's ad is often the nudge that gets them to finally ask. Pictures of real work in their area help, because the homeowner is deciding whether to trust you before they've ever talked to you.

Urgent work behaves differently. When a storm drops a tree on a fence, homeowners search and call. That's Google's territory, and it's why many tree companies run both.

When Facebook ads aren't worth it

  • When nobody can respond for hours. If leads sit until the end of the day, most of the money spent getting them is wasted.
  • When your crews are already booked out. More leads only help if there's room to do the work.
  • When the budget is too small to learn anything. A few dollars a day produces a handful of leads a month, which isn't enough to tell whether it's working. How to set a budget.

Questions about Facebook leads

Are Facebook leads good for tree service companies?

They can be. They come from people who weren't searching for a tree company, so interest fades fast. They work when each lead gets a text and a call within minutes and is booked onto a specific time, and when you pay for estimates that happen instead of every form.

Why don't Facebook leads answer the phone?

Usually because the call came too late. The homeowner filled out the form in a few taps while scrolling, and their interest lasts minutes, not hours. A text in the company's name right away, then a call, reaches far more of them than a callback the next day.

Is Google or Facebook better for tree service ads?

They do different jobs. Google catches people searching right now, which suits urgent work like storm damage. Facebook reaches people who need work done but weren't looking, which suits trimming, palms, stumps and removals homeowners have been putting off. Many tree companies run both.

How do you stop paying for tire kickers?

Respond within minutes, book a specific time in the first conversation, ask enough on the form to sort out jobs you won't take, and pay for shown estimates instead of leads. With per-shown-estimate billing, a lead that never books costs you ad spend but no fee.

What tree jobs work best with Facebook ads?

Jobs homeowners know they need but keep putting off: limbs over the roof, palm trimming, stump grinding and dead trees. A local company's ad with real photos of its own work is often the nudge that gets them to ask.
